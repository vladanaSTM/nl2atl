1

The problem

This part is short. Two questions really: why does this problem matter, and why had nobody solved it yet?

Motivation

Writing the specification is the bottleneck

  • Model checking is only as good as the formula you give it.
  • Writing correct strategic specs needs a rare expert.
  • Requirements start as ambiguous natural language; verification needs the opposite.

You all live this every day. A model checker that runs flawlessly on the wrong formula just hands you a confident, wrong answer. The hard part was never the checking. It's writing a specification that's actually correct.

And for multi-agent systems it gets harder still, because the spec has to pin down three things at once: which coalition, can force what, and by when.

Now, where do requirements come from in the first place? People. Written in English, which is ambiguous by its very nature. Ambiguous in context, ambiguous in grammar. And verification needs the exact opposite of that. So really, our job is to recover the formula the engineer had in mind.

The task, concretely

One sentence → one formula

"The system and the manager together can guarantee that sooner or later the goal is reached."
<<System,Manager>> F goal_reached
<<…>>
the coalition & its strategy
F
temporal goal: "eventually"
goal_reached
atomic proposition

Read the formula in three pieces. The double angle brackets are the coalition operator: this group has a strategy to force the outcome, no matter what everyone else does. The F is the temporal part: eventually, sooner or later. And the last piece is just the proposition, the thing we're claiming happens.

Notice how neatly that lines up with who, force what, and when from the slide before. That's the entire task right there. English goes in, a formula like this comes out. And keep an eye on that one little detail: "sooner or later" turned into F. English has a dozen ways to say each operator, and that is going to matter enormously later on.

ATL* is the same idea with more freedom in where the temporal operators can sit. I'll leave the precise semantics to the paper. Again, that's your home turf, not mine.

Why it's new

Temporal logics: done. Strategic logics: not.

Target logicNL → logic?Dataset?
LTL / STL / CTLyesyes
ATL / ATL* (strategic)nonenone
No model, no dataset, no tool for the strategic, multi-agent case.

So what's already out there? If you want to translate English into temporal logic, there's a decent body of work. Mostly LTL, a little STL and CTL. But all of it is single-agent, or purely temporal.

The moment you add the strategic dimension, coalitions, what a group can force against an adversary, the shelf is bare. No trained model, no public dataset, no tool. Nothing at all. That's the gap we set out to fill.

And one deliberate choice worth flagging. Those existing LTL datasets tend to keep the English hugging the formula, almost word for word. We pushed hard the other way, on purpose: more paraphrase, more ambiguity. Let me show you what I mean.

What makes it hard

Ambiguity: one sentence, two required readings

"Every rover can guarantee it never enters a hazardous area."

distributive — each rover, its own area

<<Rover1>>G!hazard_1 && … && <<Rover3>>G!hazard_3

collective — the coalition, one area

<<Rover1,Rover2,Rover3>>G!hazard
Both readings are gold — the model must produce both.

Read the sentence: "Every rover can guarantee it never enters a hazardous area." Sounds perfectly clear, doesn't it? It isn't. "Every rover," "a hazardous area" — does each rover avoid its own area, or do they all steer clear of one shared area? Those are two genuinely different formulas. On the left, the distributive reading. On the right, collective.

And here's our stance. Because the sentence really is ambiguous, we don't make the model choose. Both readings are the correct answer, and the model has to produce both of them. That little idea, that the answer can be a whole set of formulas, comes back to bite us in the evaluation. So file it away.

There are five of these phenomena in the dataset: dislocation, ellipsis, right-node-raising, quantifier scope. I'm only showing you one. And this one, quantifier scope, stays the single hardest thing even after we fine-tune.

If somebody drills me on the exact linguistics, that's my co-authors' design, and I'll happily connect you. My job starts one step later: the model has to notice the ambiguity and hand back more than one reading.

The dataset

The foundation everything else rests on.

I'll keep this one short, since it's my co-authors' work. But nothing I do later is possible without it.

The resource

1100 expert-written NL ↔ ATL/ATL* pairs

  • Hand-written by two experts, validated by three.
  • First dataset for NL → strategic logic.
  • Balanced operators; five ambiguity families.
1100
instances

Eleven hundred pairs. Every single one hand-written by two domain experts, then checked by three more, with their reasoning written down. Nothing scraped off the web, nothing spat out by a template. That's the reason we can actually trust the gold labels.

It's balanced across the temporal operators, and each operator shows up phrased lots of different ways, so a model can't sneak through by matching keywords. Five families of ambiguity, split fairly evenly.

Why does this matter to me? Simple. No dataset, no fine-tuning. This is the thing that makes "take a small local model and specialise it" possible in the first place. And yes, eleven hundred sounds small. Stay with me — I'll show you why it's enough.

One design choice that matters later

The gold answer can be a set of formulas

  • Ambiguous inputs have several jointly-required formulas.
  • "Correct" = produced all of them.
  • This shapes the training target, the exact-match check, and the judge.

Tiny slide, but it echoes through the whole rest of the talk. For an ambiguous item, the gold answer is the full set of readings, and you need all of them.

That single decision quietly turns up everywhere downstream. Training stitches all the readings into one target. Exact match demands every one of them. The judge demands every one of them. Same rule, three places: produce the whole set, not just a slice of it. Tuck that away for the evaluation section.

The framework

Now the system that actually does the translating. This is the biggest chunk of the talk, and the heart of my engineering work. I'll unpack the machine-learning pieces as they come up, so don't worry if a term is new to you.

Architecture

One pipeline, local and cloud behind one interface

Let's read it top to bottom. A request arrives, from the command line, a REST call, or the model checker itself. The orchestrator picks which data split and which model to use.

Two things here I really want you to notice, because they're what make the whole comparison fair. First, a local open-weight model and a cloud API sit behind the exact same interface. A 3-billion-parameter model on my GPU and GPT-5.4 over the network look completely identical to the rest of the pipeline. Second, every single generation gets saved raw, with all its metadata, before anything is allowed to score it. So scoring can never quietly leak back into generation. And months later I can re-grade everything without paying to re-run a single model.

Below that: two evaluators, exact match and an LLM judge, and then aggregation. Everything under "raw predictions" is really the next section — evaluation.

Why open-weight

Local first — requirements are confidential

  • Open-weight models fine-tuned and run on-premises.
  • Cloud API through the same interface, when data sensitivity allows.
Industrial requirements are IP — often they can't leave the building at all.

Here's the practical reason any of this open-weight work matters. A company's requirements spell out exactly what their system is meant to do. That is sensitive intellectual property, and often it's regulated on top of that. So "just send your safety requirements off to a US cloud API" — for a lot of organisations, that's simply a non-starter.

That's why we build local-first, with the cloud as an optional path behind the same interface. And it turns the whole thing into a real research question: can a small model on your own machine genuinely keep up with the giants? That's what the results section is going to answer.

The experiment grid

Two knobs, four conditions

zero-shotfew-shot

Few-shot: examples in the prompt, weights frozen.

Two knobs, turned independently. Knob one is the prompt: zero-shot, meaning just instructions, or few-shot, meaning a handful of worked examples dropped into the prompt. Knob two is the weights: leave the model as it shipped, or fine-tune it on our training data. Two by two, four conditions.

One distinction to hold on to while you read the results. Few-shot is learning in-context, the weights never move. Fine-tuning actually rewrites the model. And the proprietary models only get the top row, the baselines, because their weights are locked away and our data is far too small to fine-tune something that size anyway. So the fight that really matters is this: fine-tune a small open model, versus few-shot a big closed one.

The AI part worth explaining

Cheap fine-tuning: LoRA / QLoRA

W′ = Wfrozen + B·A

train only the small B, A (low rank)

  • Freeze the model; learn a tiny low-rank patch.
  • QLoRA: 4-bit base → a 7B model on one GPU.
  • Fits a narrow domain with modest data.

I'm guessing most of you don't fine-tune models for a living, so let me make this quick and painless. The model already speaks English, and it already knows syntax that looks a lot like logic. We're not teaching it something brand new. We're just nudging it toward our precise ATL conventions. It's a small adjustment, so there's no reason to move all the weights.

That's the idea behind LoRA. You freeze the big weight matrix, and you bolt on a small correction, two skinny matrices, B times A. Because they're low-rank, together they come to only a few million numbers, and those numbers are all we ever train. Picture a little patch stuck on top of a frozen base.

QLoRA goes one step further and stores that frozen base in 4-bit, so a 7-billion model fine-tunes on a single GPU. That's the difference between "book time on a cluster" and "run it in the lab this afternoon." And with just 765 training examples, that small capacity is actually a gift: it picks up our format without overfitting to the data.

From formula to verification

Wired into the VITAMIN model checker

  • Translation as a small service; a light patch calls it.
  • Falls back to native generation if it's unavailable.
  • The checker's own parser rejects anything ill-formed.

Here's the whole loop, end to end. A user types the English requirement straight into the model checker, hits generate, and back comes our running example: System and Manager, eventually the goal is reached. The very one from the title slide.

The translator runs as its own separate service. That keeps it swappable, and it means it can never destabilise the checker. The patch is non-destructive, and if the service is down, the tool just falls back to its own generation as if nothing happened.

And here's the part that should reassure this room in particular. I don't need you to trust that the model always produces valid syntax. The checker's parser is the gatekeeper. Anything malformed gets thrown out before verification even begins. The worst case is "no formula" — never a formula that's silently broken.

Let me be honest about scope, though. This is a working integration, not a full user study. That one's on the future-work list.

Measuring "correct"

So how do you actually score a translation? Sounds trivial. It really isn't — and if you get it wrong, every number in this talk is worthless.

The measurement problem

Exact string match punishes correct answers

gold

<<Doctor>> F discharged

prediction

<<Doctor>> F patient_discharged
≠

Same meaning. Different string.
Exact match says wrong.

Look at these two. The model wrote "patient_discharged" where the gold says just "discharged." Same coalition, same operator, same meaning. Any human glances at it and says, yeah, that's the same thing. Exact string match says: wrong.

And this is not some rare edge case. It happens constantly. Propositions pulled from the sentence can be named a dozen equivalent ways, conjunctions come back in a different order, Booleans have equivalent forms. So exact match quietly, systematically undersells how good the models really are. And here's the spoiler: it undersells the proprietary models the most, because they paraphrase the most.

So we keep exact match, but only as a strict floor. We need something on top that actually reads for meaning. And remember this pair, "discharged" versus "patient_discharged." It walks right back on stage in the results.

Why not check equivalence formally?

It doesn't fit this setting

  • No shared model. Propositions & agents are grounded in the sentence, not a fixed game structure — discharged and patient_discharged are different symbols to a checker.
  • Cost. ATL* equivalence is expensive anyway.
We check the right thing: is the strategic-temporal intent preserved?

Now, this room's instinct is going to fire immediately: "you people have tools for formula equivalence, just use those." I wish. It doesn't work here, and it's worth a minute on why.

Equivalence is only ever defined relative to a model. But our atoms and agent names come straight out of the English sentence, not some shared game structure. To a checker, "discharged" and "patient_discharged" are simply two different symbols, and nothing on earth tells it they point to the same thing, because that knowledge lives in the language, not the logic. There's no common model to check them over. And even if there were, ATL* equivalence is expensive.

So instead we aim at what we genuinely care about: did the translation keep the intent? The coalition, the modality and whether it's positive or negative, the operators, the scope. That is exactly what a human reviewer checks by eye, and it's something a language model can approximate. We're trading a formal guarantee for a validated estimate, and the next few slides are entirely about how far you can trust that estimate. The really deep complexity questions, I'm happy to punt on.

The meaning-level check

LLM-as-a-judge — only where exact match failed

prediction→ exact match?→ yes = correct
prediction↓ no judge: same intent?→ correct / not
accuracy = exact-match floor  +  judge-recovered

Notice that the judge only ever sees the predictions that already failed exact match. A string match is accepted automatically. So the judge can only ever rescue things from below the floor. It can never overrule a match. That deliberately caps how much of the final number rests on soft judgement.

It works from a fixed rubric: coalition, modality and polarity, the temporal operators, the scope, and for the ambiguous items, are all the readings there. It hands back a verdict plus a one-line reason, so every call is auditable. And we always report the split — this much came from exact match, this much the judge rescued. We are not hiding anything behind that word "judge."

Who judges the judge?

Six judges, checked against human experts

  • Six independent LLM judges, one rubric.
  • Expert audit of 599 predictions: two annotators, κ = 0.99.
  • Headline uses the most human-aligned judge.

judges benchmarked

DeepSeek GPT-4.1 GPT-5.2 GPT-5.4 Gemma-27B Llama-70B

An LLM grading other LLMs — I know, it sounds hopelessly circular. So our answer is not "trust us." Our answer is that we checked the judges against real humans, and we used six of them.

Here's the ground truth. Our experts audited around 600 predictions by hand, deliberately loading up on the cases where the judges disagreed with each other. Two annotators, agreeing 99.5% of the time, kappa of 0.99. They almost never disagree with one another, and that is exactly what makes their verdict solid bedrock.

Against that bedrock we rank all six judges by how closely they track the humans, and we report our headline numbers using the single best-aligned one. Nice bonus: that judge isn't one of the models we're grading, so it never marks its own homework. And which judge actually wins — that's the twist, and it's coming up in the results.

Here's the table. Top of it, the open-weight Llama-70B. Kappa 0.59, agreeing with humans 82% of the time. Clearly the best of the bunch. And down at the bottom? GPT-5.2 at 0.16, GPT-5.4 at 0.24. That's precisely why our headline uses Llama — it tracks humans best, and it never grades its own output. And on the next slide I'll show you why this happens, because the reason is almost embarrassingly simple.

Why — and it's simple

The strong models are pedantic about paraphrases

discharged # gold  vs  patient_discharged # prediction — same structure
  • On 1167 items GPT-5.4 & GPT-5.2 rejected what Llama accepted.
  • Humans sided with Llama 160 to 26; zero the other way.
  • GPT-5.4 even marks down its own outputs (0.63 vs 0.86).

When you actually open up what the strong judges rejected, it's almost all one single thing: predicate aliasing. The prediction renames an atom to a paraphrase that's grounded right there in the sentence — "discharged" becomes "patient_discharged" — while every coalition, every operator, every connective stays identical. Our rubric says plainly: accept clear aliases like that. The strong models reject them anyway, insisting it's "not a clearly grounded alias." And the humans side with the lenient judge, overwhelmingly.

And I can put numbers on "overwhelmingly." On 1,167 items, the two strict proprietary judges rejected something Llama had accepted. On the slice our experts audited, the humans backed Llama 160 to 26 — and not once did it go the other way. So this isn't Llama being sloppy and waving things through. It's the strong models being over-strict.

One more, and this is the clincher, because you might suspect Llama is just flattering its own kind. It isn't. GPT-5.4, acting as judge, marks down GPT-5.4's own translations too, scoring them 0.63 where Llama gives 0.86. So the strict judges punish everybody, and they punish the paraphrase-heavy proprietary generators the hardest. Only a well-calibrated judge lets their true accuracy show. And the lesson travels far beyond this paper: if you ever use an LLM as a judge, do not assume the biggest model is the best judge. Check it against humans first.

Wrap-up

Home stretch. The limits, what we're giving away, and then I really want your questions.

Honest limits

What this doesn't do

  • Curated, English-only dataset — not in-the-wild requirements.
  • The metric is a validated estimate (judge–human κ is moderate).
  • Confidence intervals cover the seed, not test-set resampling (~±0.06).
  • Scope is ATL/ATL* — not Strategy Logic or epistemic logics.
  • Equivalence by intent, not by formal model checking.

Let me not oversell any of this. Two limits matter most. The dataset is curated, so what you've seen is a controlled study, not a field deployment. And our metric is a validated estimate: judge-human agreement is only moderate, so treat these numbers as well-calibrated, not exact to the last decimal.

I'll be straight about the statistics too. Our confidence intervals vary the training seed at one fixed test split. But with 218 test items, resampling the test set itself adds around 0.06 of wobble that those intervals just don't capture. So any gap smaller than about 0.06 is genuinely not resolvable from this data — which is exactly why I keep saying "tie" and never "winner." The framework can run k-fold to measure that properly; we left the full sweep for future runs.

The scope question, and the equivalence-by-intent point, I'll hand to the co-authors if anyone wants to push on them.

Backup · judge prompt

What the judge is asked

  • Same strategic-temporal intent? Check coalition, modality & polarity, operators, scope.
  • For ambiguous inputs: all readings present.
  • Accept input-grounded aliases; reject real meaning changes.
  • Return a label + a short reason. Fixed prompt, all six judges, cached per judge.

The rubric just walks the intent components one by one, and it says in black and white: accept clear aliases. That's the exact instruction the strong proprietary judges ignored. That one line is the whole of result two.

Averaging in the strict judges lowers everything and nudges open-weight ahead (Δ≈0.05, p=0.03) — but that lead is the strict judges over-rejecting proprietary paraphrases. Under the human-aligned judge it's a tie. Honest reading: parity.

This is my answer if someone accuses me of cherry-picking Llama. Average all six judges instead, and the worst case for the proprietary models is still a tie — and the little open-weight lead you see here is just the over-strictness artifact creeping back in. If anything, the headline judge is the conservative choice for our own claim, not the flattering one.
