We report an empirical study on the held-out test split using the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The questions are practical: can LLMs translate natural language into strategic temporal logic reliably enough to feed a verifier, and how do locally deployable open-weight models compare with proprietary baselines?

\paragraph{Setting.}
We evaluate six models across four conditions, yielding $20$ model--condition systems. Two proprietary Azure OpenAI models, \textsc{gpt-4.1} and \textsc{gpt-5.4}, are run zero- and few-shot as strong baselines: being closed-weight, they cannot be adapted within the on-premises setting that motivates our framework, and the modest in-domain supervision makes API-side fine-tuning at their scale impractical, so they serve as strong \emph{few-shot} reference points. Our central comparison thus contrasts in-domain fine-tuning of small open-weight models with few-shot prompting of much larger proprietary ones, rather than model class alone. Four open-weight models---\textsc{mistral-7b}, \textsc{qwen-3b}, \textsc{phi3} (Phi-3.5-mini), and \textsc{qwen-coder-7b}---are run as baselines and after LoRA fine-tuning, in both prompting conditions. All systems share identical prompt templates, few-shot exemplars, and decoding (one greedy decode per query, with no chain-of-thought, self-consistency, or parser-feedback/self-repair scaffolding for any model), with local fine-tuning and inference on NVIDIA A100 GPUs, so the comparison isolates in-domain fine-tuning versus few-shot prompting rather than inference-time engineering applied unevenly to one side. The parser canonicalizes surface form identically before scoring, and the tool's parser-validated fallback (Section~\ref{subsec:integration}) is a model-agnostic deployment feature held out of this controlled comparison; the proprietary baselines thus receive the same best-effort prompting as the open-weight systems, and the reported accuracies are a conservative floor that such scaffolding could lift for all alike. Of the $1100$ instances, the $7$ curated few-shot exemplars are held out of every split; the remaining $1093$ are partitioned by a stratified $70/10/20$ split into $765$ training, $110$ validation, and $218$ test examples. Each fine-tuned configuration is repeated over three training seeds, and every prediction is scored by four LLM judges (DeepSeek-V3.2, GPT-4.1, GPT-5.2, and GPT-5.4) plus an expert human audit on a stratified subsample, for $4{,}279$ items with multiple judge verdicts.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning versus few-shot baselines.}
Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} reports semantic accuracy by model and condition under the human-aligned judge. The top is a statistically tied pair of fine-tuned open-weight systems with few-shot prompting, \textsc{qwen-coder-7b} ($0.734$) and \textsc{phi3} ($0.722$), both clearly above the strongest few-shot proprietary baseline, \textsc{gpt-5.4} few-shot ($0.662$). Fine-tuning is the dominant factor: it lifts \textsc{phi3} from $0.275$ to $0.722$ in the few-shot setting and moves every open-weight model from very weak zero-shot baselines (as low as $0.046$) into the $0.62$--$0.73$ band. Few-shot prompting helps throughout, but its benefit is largest for the untuned baselines and shrinks after fine-tuning, so task adaptation, rather than scale alone, drives performance: the $3$--$7$B fine-tuned models surpass much larger \emph{few-shot} proprietary systems. These gains come from fine-tuning on the $765$-instance training split, augmented twofold by templated paraphrasing of the input (synonym substitutions for temporal and strategic phrasings that leave the gold formula unchanged) applied to the training split only, so validation and test are never augmented; modest in-domain supervision thus suffices to specialize a small model to ATL/ATL$^\ast$. The $95\%$ seed confidence intervals for the top systems are narrow (about $\pm0.02$; see the two-judge robustness check in the technical appendix), but they capture only training-seed variation at the fixed canonical split and \emph{not} test-set sampling variance, which at $n{=}218$ items is on the order of $\pm0.06$; the seed intervals are therefore a lower bound, and a stratified $k$-fold sweep, which our framework supports, would estimate the split component directly and is left to future runs.

\paragraph{Exact match versus meaning.}
We decompose each headline system's accuracy under the human-aligned judge into a deterministic exact-match floor and the fraction the LLM judge recovers (the full per-system breakdown, under the conservative two-judge mean, is in the technical appendix). The gap between syntactic and meaning-level scoring is large and uneven. For \textsc{gpt-5.4} zero-shot, exact match yields only $0.213$ of the $0.412$ accuracy: roughly $48\%$ of correct answers are syntactically distinct from the reference yet strategically equivalent; for \textsc{gpt-4.1} few-shot the judge-recovered share is $23\%$. It is smaller for the strongest fine-tuned systems ($12$--$18\%$), whose outputs more often match the reference surface form, but never negligible. Purely syntactic metrics therefore systematically understate translation quality, especially on underspecified requirements.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.}
A paired analysis on shared items confirms a tight top cluster. On the conservative two-judge metric, the pairwise differences among the fine-tuned \textsc{phi3} and \textsc{qwen-coder-7b} systems (few- and zero-shot; all $\le 0.021$) are not significant (randomization $p>0.15$), whereas the cluster's margin over the next tier (\textsc{qwen-3b}) is significant ($p<0.02$). The lead over the strongest few-shot proprietary baseline is likewise significant: fine-tuned \textsc{phi3} few-shot exceeds \textsc{gpt-5.4} few-shot by $0.083$ on shared test items (paired bootstrap $95\%$ CI $[0.03,0.14]$, randomization $p<0.01$); the lead persists under the human-aligned judge of Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} ($0.722$ vs.\ $0.662$). Several fine-tuned open-weight configurations thus form a statistically equivalent top group, clearly separated from both the rest and the few-shot proprietary baselines.

\paragraph{Judge reliability.}
Table~\ref{tab:judge_reliability} summarizes inter-rater reliability. The four LLM judges agree substantially with \emph{one another} (mean pairwise Cohen's $\kappa=0.51$) yet track the human audit very unevenly, and judge quality does \emph{not} follow model capability or recency. \textsc{gpt-5.4}, the strongest few-shot \emph{generator} in Table~\ref{tab:accuracy}, is only a \emph{fair} judge ($\kappa=0.24$ against humans), and \textsc{gpt-5.2} is worse ($\kappa=0.16$, slight), both routinely rejecting predictions that merely rename an agent; the most human-aligned judge is instead the less recent \textsc{DeepSeek-V3.2} ($\kappa=0.46$, moderate), slightly ahead of \textsc{gpt-4.1} ($\kappa=0.44$, moderate). We therefore report headline accuracy under \textsc{DeepSeek-V3.2}, which is both the best-aligned judge and external to the evaluated systems. The conclusion is robust to this choice: the conservative two-judge mean (technical appendix) lowers the absolute numbers by $0.03$--$0.05$, while a four-judge mean (which additionally lets the two proprietary baselines grade their own outputs) raises them slightly (top cluster \textsc{phi3} $0.711$, \textsc{qwen-coder-7b} $0.709$, vs.\ \textsc{gpt-5.4} $0.649$); under every judge the fine-tuned open-weight systems stay the top cluster, above both few-shot proprietary baselines. When human labels override the judges wherever an item was audited, the top cluster is unchanged and only $6$ of $20$ systems shift rank, all among adjacent neighbors.

\paragraph{Efficiency.}
We characterize efficiency through the accuracy--latency trade-off, plotted in the technical appendix. The two fastest fine-tuned open-weight systems are also among the most accurate: few-shot \textsc{qwen-3b} ($1567$\,ms) and \textsc{phi3} ($1681$\,ms), both below the proprietary baselines \textsc{gpt-4.1} ($1979$\,ms) and \textsc{gpt-5.4} ($2538$\,ms); few-shot \textsc{qwen-coder-7b} trades latency for the best open-weight accuracy ($3338$\,ms), and fine-tuned \textsc{mistral-7b} is slowest ($4460$\,ms). The Pareto frontier is thus occupied entirely by fine-tuned open-weight models. The proprietary baselines are dominated on accuracy; their latency is network-timed and only indicative across the API/local boundary, but the accuracy comparison alone already favors local deployment.

\paragraph{Robustness to content filtering.}
The Azure safety filter blocked only two inputs (a Shakespeare quotation and one other), counted as incorrect; excluding them leaves every system's rank unchanged, so no conclusion hinges on provider-side filtering.

\paragraph{Error analysis by ambiguity type.}
The failure modes cluster by linguistic phenomenon. The dominant errors of the \emph{untuned} open-weight models are syntactic: predictions borrow CTL-style path quantifiers (e.g.\ \texttt{AF\,p}) for the coalition modality, introduce first-order quantifiers and variables foreign to ATL/ATL$^\ast$, or emit malformed coalitions, all rejected by both exact match and the judges. Fine-tuning largely removes these gross errors, accounting for the steep zero-shot-to-fine-tuned jump. The residual errors concentrate on the ambiguity families: under quantifier-scope ambiguity the models most often \emph{collapse} the distributive and collective readings into one formula or return only one of the two required; under VP ellipsis and Right Node Raising they fail to \emph{reconstruct} the shared objective for the second agent; and dislocation occasionally yields a mis-attributed coalition. Temporal-operator substitutions (e.g.\ \texttt{U} for \texttt{F}) and spurious or dropped negations occur across categories but are rare once a model is fine-tuned. These patterns track the benchmark's design intent: once surface syntax is mastered, the discriminating difficulty is recovering the intended \emph{scope} and \emph{distribution} of strategic ability, not lexical translation. The technical appendix makes this quantitative on the $31$ multi-reading (QSA) and $187$ single-reading test items (a re-aggregation of existing verdicts, no new labels). Single-reading accuracy is uniformly high ($0.63$--$0.75$), but the QSA slice is where the systems separate: the proprietary few-shot baselines almost never return both readings (\textsc{gpt-4.1} $0.03$, \textsc{gpt-5.4} $0.07$), whereas all four fine-tuned open-weight models at least partially acquire the distributive/collective convention (QSA accuracy $0.23$--$0.48$). With only $31$ QSA items the per-system differences \emph{within} the fine-tuned group are within noise (binomial $95\%$ CI $\approx\pm0.17$), so we read this slice as a qualitative contrast (fine-tuned models begin to handle the convention, the proprietary baselines do not) rather than a ranking. The QSA slice is nonetheless the single largest source of the proprietary-baseline gap, and a key reason the fine-tuned open-weight models lead overall.

\paragraph{Discussion.}
Taken together, our experiments show that in-domain fine-tuning of small open-weight models translates strategic requirements at least as accurately as strong few-shot proprietary baselines while keeping requirements on-premises, and that exact match alone is an inadequate metric. Complete tables, the full training and decoding configuration, the computing infrastructure, and a reproducibility checklist appear in the technical appendix and supplementary material.
